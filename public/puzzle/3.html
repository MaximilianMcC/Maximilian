The Remuera railway station opened in 1873. It was part of Auckland's first railway, which was the Onehunga line. Today, the station sits on the south-city line and the Onehunga-West line. The line did both passenger and goods services, but now the station is used for passenger workings only. KiwiRail still do operate on the tracks though, and if you're lucky you might see them drive a train through.

The main attraction of Remuera's station is no doubt the signal box that still survives. This is a very rare site to see in New Zealand. Apparently it has gone through a full cosmetic restoration which is excellent to hear. Signal boxes are small buildings that house many levers which are used to control both the position of the signals, and the position of the track.

Most of the world, including New Zealand, uses the absolute block way of signalling. This is where the railway is split up into a lot of different sections, called blocks. Each signalman is given his own block. The rule is that only one train may be in a block at a time.

Signalmen communicate with each other to ensure the safe running of trains. Signal boxes were invented far before the telephone, so different bell codes are used to send information between the two boxes. These bells were electronically operated. The pulse would go through the telegraph wires to the next box. Even after the invention of the telephone, bell codes was the standard way to signal trains, and in some places in the world you can find them still in operation. Did you know that the railways were the first practical use of electricity? I think it is very interesting how the railway manages to link back to everything somehow. There really was no greater invention.

These bell codes were often sent using things called block instruments. These contained the bells, as well as the button to send rings. Some fancy ones also included block indicators which told the signalman if there was a train on the block before/ahead of his box. Block instruments are perhaps the earliest example of feasible long-distance instant communication, even if you could only ever talk about trains. For single-tracked railways, the block instruments also dispensed tablets. These were used to keep track of what train was allowed where. It's best to think of tablets as keys, as that is exactly what they were.

These bell codes don't exactly let you say whatever you want, instead there was a strict set of patterns that would mean different things. The job of a signalman is to remember these patterns, because if a mistake were ever made the consequences could be disastrous. Most accidents on the railway are from the result of improper signalling practises. In the modern day most signalling is done from one central place. All of Auckland's trains are signalled from a random building in Ellerslie.

New Zealand used to have a giant fleet of steam locomotives that went all over the country. Unfortunately our modern day rail network is quite small. It is good to see that it is coming back to strength though with the introduction of new services such as Te Huia and the opening of the CRL.
